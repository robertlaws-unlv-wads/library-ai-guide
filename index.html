Working with AI, carefully

A self-paced module for UNLV students and staff on what generative AI is actually doing, where it breaks, and how to make good decisions about using it. About 40 minutes. You can stop and come back at any point.

Before we start, here is something to look at

Someone asked an AI research assistant a straightforward question: what does the research say about how first-year students evaluate sources? This is part of what came back.

AI-generated response

Research on first-year students' source evaluation has grown considerably over the past decade. Nguyen (2021) found that students who received embedded library instruction were substantially more likely to consult peer-reviewed sources than those who did not. This aligns with broader findings that roughly 68% of first-year students cannot reliably distinguish a scholarly article from a popular one. Wineburg and McGrew (2019) demonstrated that lateral reading — leaving a page to check what other sources say about it — is a markedly more effective strategy than close reading of the page itself.

It reads well. The citations are formatted correctly. One of those authors is real and the finding attributed to them is real. One of the papers does not exist. One statistic has no source at all. And one citation is completely genuine but is being used to support a claim its authors never made.

Spotting which is which is a skill, and it is a skill you can learn in about ten minutes. That is roughly what this module is for. We will come back to this passage in Section 2 and take it apart line by line.

Nothing here is recorded

There is no login, no account, and no tracking. Your answers are worked out in your own browser and are never sent anywhere — not to the Libraries, not to your instructor, not to your supervisor. Nothing is saved between visits, which also means closing a tab loses your place in the quiz.

We are asking you to think carefully about where your data goes, so it would be strange not to tell you where yours goes here. If you would like to confirm it rather than take our word for it, open your browser's developer tools: the Network tab shows no third-party requests, and Application shows no cookies and no stored data. That is deliberate.

By the end, you should be able to

  • Explain, in your own words, why a fluent and confident answer can still be wrong.
  • Take a specific AI-generated claim and describe how you would verify it.
  • Decide whether a given piece of information is safe to put into a given tool.
  • Tell the difference between being permitted to use AI, disclosing that you used it, and citing it.
  • Work through an unfamiliar situation where the right answer is not obvious.

What is in the module

  1. What you're actually talking to 6 min

    Why these systems produce fluent text rather than looked-up facts, and why the chatbot and the model behind it are not the same thing.

  2. When it makes things up 8 min

    Fabricated sources, real sources misused, and five verification moves you can actually remember. Includes the passage above.

  3. Whose world does it describe? 5 min

    How bias gets into these systems, who it lands on, and why AI writing detectors are a live problem at this university.

  4. What not to paste 6 min

    One question to ask before you paste anything, and what changes when you are using a tool UNLV holds a contract for.

  5. Using it honestly 6 min

    Permission, disclosure, and citation are three separate things. Plus what to do when someone is accused by a detector.

  6. Deciding when to use it 6 min

    Four questions that work on situations nobody has written a rule for yet, tested against three real scenarios.

  7. Getting better output 4 min

    What actually improves results, and which popular prompting advice is superstition.

  8. Quiz 8 min

    Ten situations rather than ten definitions. Every answer is explained, including the wrong ones.

  9. What to take with you 2 min

    A one-page summary built to be printed and kept.

Who this is for

Students and staff, in the same module. That is a deliberate choice. The situations differ — one of you is worried about a syllabus, the other about a patron at a service desk — but the underlying judgment is the same, and each group benefits from seeing the other's position. Staff get asked these questions at the desk. Students are the ones being accused by detectors. Each section ends with the same principle shown from both sides.

No prior technical knowledge is assumed, and nothing here assumes you are either a novice or an expert on account of your age or your job title.

How this relates to the Libraries' other AI guide

The Libraries already publishes AI 101, an introductory guide covering foundational concepts, machine learning, ethics, and current issues. If you want the conceptual grounding — what machine learning is, how the field got here — start there. This module is the applied companion: shorter, hands-on, and built around practice rather than explanation. You do not need to have read AI 101 first.

What this module does not cover

AI for writing code, research data management, IRB and human-subjects questions, grant writing, and accessibility accommodations are all out of scope. This module also does not override your syllabus, your instructor, or your supervisor — where a local rule exists, it wins. Ask a librarian if you need a pointer to the right person.

What you will learn

This module is for UNLV students and staff who want to use these tools without getting caught out by them.

Not a tool catalogue. AI literacy here is the judgment to know what a system can do, where it fails, and what the university expects of you. The Libraries have always taught source evaluation — this is the same skill, applied to a new kind of source.

  • 01

    Understand what you are talking to

    Why these systems produce fluent text rather than looked-up facts, and why a confident answer is not a more reliable one.

  • 02

    Verify what it tells you

    Five named checks you can actually remember, built for the failure that matters now: real sources described wrongly.

  • 03

    Decide what is safe to share

    One question to ask before you paste anything, and what changes when the tool is one UNLV holds a contract for.

  • 04

    Say what you did

    Permission, disclosure and citation are three separate obligations. Most confusion comes from treating them as one.

Before we start

Here is something to look at

Someone asked an AI research assistant a straightforward question: what does the research say about how first-year students evaluate sources? This is part of what came back.

AI-generated response

Research on first-year students' source evaluation has grown considerably over the past decade. Nguyen (2021) found that students who received embedded library instruction were substantially more likely to consult peer-reviewed sources than those who did not (College & Research Libraries, 82(3), 411–428). This aligns with broader findings that roughly 68% of first-year students cannot reliably distinguish a scholarly article from a popular one. Wineburg and McGrew (2019) demonstrated that lateral reading — leaving a page to check what other sources say about it — is a markedly more effective strategy than close reading of the page itself. As Head and Eisenberg's Project Information Literacy work established, students who use library databases go on to earn higher course grades. A 2024 meta-analysis in the Journal of Academic Librarianship synthesised 340 studies on information literacy instruction (doi:10.1016/j.acalib.2024.102847).

It reads well and the citations are formatted correctly. Five claims are being made, and only one of them is sound. One cites a paper that does not exist. One gives a number with no source. One points at real, correctly cited research that says something different. And one has a DOI that resolves to a different article entirely.

Spotting which is which is a skill, and it takes about ten minutes to learn. We take this passage apart line by line in Section 02.

The module

Seven sections

  1. What you're actually talking to 6 min

    Why these systems produce fluent text rather than looked-up facts, and why the chatbot and the model behind it are not the same thing.

  2. When it makes things up 8 min

    Fabricated sources, real sources misused, and five verification moves you can actually remember. Includes the passage above.

  3. Whose world does it describe? 5 min

    How bias gets into these systems, who it lands on, and why AI writing detectors are a live problem at this university.

  4. What not to paste 6 min

    One question to ask before you paste anything, and what changes when you are using a tool UNLV holds a contract for.

  5. Using it honestly 6 min

    Permission, disclosure, and citation are three separate things. Plus what to do when someone is accused by a detector.

  6. Deciding when to use it 6 min

    Four questions that work on situations nobody has written a rule for yet, tested against three real scenarios.

  7. Getting better output 4 min

    What actually improves results, and which popular prompting advice is superstition.

  8. Quiz 8 min

    Ten situations rather than ten definitions. Every answer is explained, including the wrong ones.

  9. What to take with you 2 min

    A one-page summary built to be printed and kept.

Nothing here is recorded

There is no login, no account, and no tracking. Your answers are worked out in your own browser and are never sent anywhere — not to the Libraries, not to your instructor, not to your supervisor. Nothing is saved between visits, which also means closing a tab loses your place in the quiz.

We are asking you to think carefully about where your data goes, so it would be strange not to tell you where yours goes here. If you would like to confirm it rather than take our word for it, open your browser's developer tools: the Network tab shows no third-party requests, and Application shows no cookies and no stored data. That is deliberate.

What this module does not cover

AI for writing code, research data management, IRB and human-subjects questions, grant writing, and accessibility accommodations are all out of scope. This module also does not override your syllabus, your instructor, or your supervisor — where a local rule exists, it wins.

How this relates to the Libraries' other AI guide

The Libraries already publishes AI 101, an introductory guide covering foundational concepts, machine learning, ethics, and current issues. If you want the conceptual grounding — what machine learning is, how the field got here — start there. This module is the applied companion: shorter, hands-on, and built around practice rather than explanation. You do not need to have read AI 101 first.

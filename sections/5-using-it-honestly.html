Using it honestly

Three separate obligations get collapsed into one and then argued about. Pulling them apart resolves most of the confusion, and most of the anxiety.

In this section

  • The difference between permission, disclosure, and citation.
  • How to write a disclosure line that takes ten seconds.
  • What to do when a detector accuses someone.

Three different questions

Permission, disclosure and citation are not the same obligation
QuestionWho decidesWhat it looks like
Permission
May I use it here?		 Your instructor, via the syllabus. Or your supervisor. Not the Libraries, and not a campus-wide rule. Checking before you start.
Disclosure
Did I say I used it?		 Usually whoever granted permission, as a condition of it. One sentence describing what the tool did.
Citation
Am I attributing content?		 Your citation style, and only if AI-generated content appears in your work. A formatted reference.

Most confusion comes from assuming these travel together. They do not. You can be permitted and still owe disclosure. You can owe disclosure with nothing to cite — which is the most common case of all, because most AI use is in the process rather than in the product.

There is no single UNLV rule

This surprises people who go looking for one. Individual instructors set expectations for their courses, so the syllabus is the authority for coursework and it varies from course to course — including between two courses you are taking this semester. UNLV's guidance asks that AI use be acknowledged and that you check each syllabus. For staff, the equivalent authority is your supervisor and your unit's practice.

Writing the disclosure

One sentence, describing what the tool did rather than that a tool existed. All of these are complete and sufficient:

I used an AI assistant to suggest alternative structures for this argument during outlining. The research, analysis, and writing are my own.

I used an AI assistant to check this draft for unclear sentences. No text was generated by it.

An AI assistant produced the first draft of the summary in section 3, which I then verified against the sources and rewrote.

Note the third one. Disclosure is not an admission of wrongdoing, and specific disclosure is more credible than vague disclosure. “AI was used in the preparation of this document” tells a reader nothing and tends to invite more questions than it settles.

Citation, when it applies

Cite when AI-generated content actually appears in your work. The major styles broadly agree on the approach and differ in the details — because the output is not retrievable by anyone else, they treat it roughly as personal communication or as software, and most recommend including the prompt. Check the current edition of the style you are using rather than relying on what you learned previously, since guidance here has been revised more than once.

The Libraries' citation guides carry current examples, and a librarian can confirm an edge case.

Check yourself

When a detector accuses someone

Section 3 covered why the scores are unreliable and who they misfire on. The practical question is what to do at the moment it happens.

If it is you: do not confess to something you did not do because a number looked convincing. Gather what shows your process — drafts, notes, outlines, document version history, browser history from your research. Ask the instructor what specifically in the work prompted the concern, which moves the conversation from a percentage to the text.

If someone brings it to you: the most useful thing you can say is that the score is not evidence and that it misfires systematically on multilingual writers. Then help them assemble the process record. Do not adjudicate whether they did it — that is not a reference-desk call, and treating it as one is costly for them.

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

Two courses, two different AI policies, and they contradict each other. That is normal and both are valid. Follow each syllabus for its own course.

At a service desk

You used AI to draft a LibGuide section. Nobody requires you to disclose that — but the Libraries' own AI 101 guide discloses its AI use, and that consistency is worth something when we are asking others to be transparent.

Key takeaways

  • Permission, disclosure, and citation are three separate obligations.
  • There is no single campus rule. The syllabus or your supervisor is the authority.
  • Specific disclosure beats vague disclosure, and one sentence is enough.
  • A detector score is not evidence. Move the conversation to the process.

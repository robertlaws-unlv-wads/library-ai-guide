Deciding when to use it

Any list of approved and forbidden tasks will be out of date by next semester. Four questions will not, because they are about the work rather than the tool.

In this section

  • Four questions that hold up in situations nobody wrote a rule for.
  • Practice applying them to three situations with no obvious answer.
  • Why over-caution has costs too.

The four questions

  1. Can I check it? If you have no way to verify the output, you have no way to use it responsibly. This is why AI is more useful in areas you already know something about — which is the opposite of how people usually reach for it.

  2. Does the judgment belong to me? Is this the part of the work you are supposed to be doing? A student asked to analyse a poem is being asked to analyse it. A librarian deciding what to weed is being trusted with that decision. Handing off the thing you were asked to do is a different act from getting help with the parts around it.

  3. Is the input mine to share? Section 4, in one line.

  4. Am I allowed, and will I say so? Section 5, in one line. Both halves — permission and disclosure are separate.

They are deliberately not a checklist that outputs a verdict. They are the questions that make your reasoning explainable, which is what you actually need when a supervisor, an instructor, or a review board asks why you did what you did.

Over-caution is also a mistake

A module like this can leave people thinking that refusing to use AI is always the safe answer. It is not always the right one. Declining legitimate help has costs — your time, your colleagues' time, and work not done — and unlike a misuse, those costs are invisible and never appear in an incident report. If the honest answer to all four questions is fine, the answer is fine. One of the three scenarios below resolves that way, on purpose.

Three situations

None of these has a single right answer, and none is scored. Pick what you would actually do, then compare the consequences across the options — the comparison is where the value is.

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

The four questions work on tasks your syllabus never anticipated — which will be most of them, because syllabi are written before the semester and tools change during it.

At a service desk

They also work when a patron asks whether they should use AI for something. You can walk through the questions with them rather than guessing at a policy that may not exist.

Key takeaways

  • Can I check it? Does the judgment belong to me? Is the input mine? Am I allowed, and will I say so?
  • They produce an answer you can explain, which is different from one you feel confident about.
  • Over-caution has real costs. Sometimes the answer is that it is fine.
  • When a request comes from above, being told to is not the same as being permitted to.

Whose world does it describe?

These systems learned from an enormous amount of human writing, which means they learned what that writing contains — including which people show up often and are described generously, and which do not.

In this section

  • How bias gets into a system nobody intended to make biased.
  • The difference between output that misrepresents and output that decides.
  • Why AI writing detectors are a live problem at this university.

Bias is inherited, not inserted

People usually picture bias as someone putting their opinions into a system. That happens occasionally and it is comparatively easy to find and argue about. The common case is duller and much harder to see: a model trained on a faithful record of an unequal world reproduces it accurately.

If most text about physicists describes men, the likely continuation after “the physicist said” reflects that. If a language has little presence online, the model is worse at it — and will still answer confidently. If historical hiring data records who got hired, a model trained on it learns who gets hired, not who should have.

Nobody decided any of this. That is the point, and it is why “the data is objective” is not a defence. Data records outcomes, and outcomes carry the conditions that produced them.

Two different kinds of harm

Worth separating, because they need different responses.

Representational harm is about how people are depicted: whose English is treated as correct, whose names get mangled or “corrected”, who appears when you ask for an image of a professor, which communities are described in flattened or stereotyped terms. It is often subtle and cumulative.

Allocative harm is about decisions made regarding people: who gets flagged, screened out, prioritised, or investigated. This is the one with immediate consequences, and it is where a biased system stops being a bad description and starts being a bad outcome.

Check yourself

Detectors, which are both kinds at once

AI writing detectors estimate whether text was machine-generated. They work by looking for statistical regularities — uniform sentence length, limited vocabulary variation, predictable structure.

Those features also describe careful writing by someone composing in an additional language. The result, found repeatedly in research on these tools, is that multilingual writers are flagged at rates many times higher than native English writers, on work they wrote themselves. Institutions have been steadily backing away from these tools as the evidence has accumulated; some have disabled them outright.

This is the clearest example in the module of the same problem landing on both audiences from opposite directions. Students are the ones accused. Staff are the ones asked what to do about it. Both need the same fact: a detector score is not evidence. It is a probability estimate from a tool with a known and unevenly distributed error rate.

If this happens to you, or to someone at your desk

The productive move is to shift the conversation from the score to the process. Drafts, outlines, notes, browser or document version history, and the ability to talk through the argument are all evidence of how the work was done. A detector percentage is not. If you need support, UNLV's Office of Student Conduct and the Libraries can both point you to the right person.

Closer to home

Library systems have their own version of this. Subject headings carry the assumptions of the era that wrote them. Discovery layers rank by signals that favour what was already well-used, which tends to mean English-language work from large publishers. Recommendation features reinforce whatever is already visible. Adding an AI layer on top of those systems does not neutralise any of it — it inherits all of it and adds fluent prose, which makes it harder to notice.

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

You ask for “the major scholars” on a topic and get a list. Ask yourself who is likely missing: work in other languages, from smaller presses, or too recent to be well cited. The list is a starting point, not a field.

At a service desk

A tool you are evaluating promises better relevance ranking. Ask the vendor what it was trained on and what it does to material that was historically under-discovered. “It learns from user behaviour” is an answer worth following up on.

Key takeaways

  • Bias is mostly inherited from data that accurately records an unequal past.
  • Separate harms of depiction from harms of decision — the second acts on people.
  • Detector scores are not evidence, and they misfire hardest on multilingual writers.
  • Shift from the score to the process: drafts, notes, version history.

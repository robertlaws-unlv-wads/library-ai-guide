Getting better output

This section is last on purpose. Learning to extract more from these tools before learning to doubt them produces people who are efficiently wrong.

In this section

  • What actually improves results.
  • Which widely-shared prompting advice is superstition.
  • A filter for evaluating the next technique you are told about.

What works

Every technique below does one of two things: gives the system information it did not have, or gives you something you can check. That is the entire list, and it is a useful test for anything else you encounter.

  • Narrow the question. “Social media and mental health” is a subject heading, not a question. Which platforms, which age group, which outcome, over what period?
  • Say who it is for and what form you want. A 300-word summary for a first-year seminar is a different object from a briefing note for a department head. Without this the system averages across every possibility.
  • Say what you already know and where you are stuck. The most underrated one. It stops the tool explaining what you understand and points it at the part you need.
  • Give an example. One example communicates structure, tone and depth faster than a paragraph describing them.
  • Ask for sources you can check — then check them. The second half is the whole value. Asking for sources makes any output look more rigorous whether or not it is.
  • Ask what it is least sure about. Its self-assessment is generated text and not a reliable measure, but it does tend to point at the thin claims, which tells you where to aim your own checking.
  • Iterate. Treat the first output as a draft to react to. “Too general, focus on the second point” usually beats rewriting the prompt from scratch.

What does not work

A large amount of circulating prompting advice is folklore. It spread because someone tried it once, the output happened to be better, and the story was memorable.

  • Telling it to be accurate. The most widespread piece of useless advice there is. Accuracy is not a setting being withheld. Worse, it tends to produce more confident-sounding text, which makes errors harder to spot.
  • Offering a tip, or claiming your job depends on it. Circulated on the back of informal experiments and does not hold up.
  • Threatening it, or being unpleasant. Does not work, and is a slightly odd habit to build.
  • Magic phrases. “Take a deep breath,” “you are a world-class expert.” Role framing has some modest effect on register; incantations do not have the effects claimed for them, and the claims are rarely tested.

A filter worth keeping

When someone tells you about a prompting trick, ask: does it add information the system did not have, or produce something I can verify? If neither, be sceptical. It costs nothing to try, but it also does nothing, and believing it works is how you stop looking for what does.

Which of these would actually help?

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

“Explain X” gets you an encyclopedia entry. “I understand X but I do not see how it applies to Y in this case” gets you the thing you were actually stuck on.

At a service desk

When a patron says the AI gave them nothing useful, the prompt is usually a topic rather than a question. This is a reference interview with a new participant, and you already know how to do it.

Key takeaways

  • Add information, or create something checkable. Everything else is decoration.
  • Audience, format, and a narrow question do most of the work.
  • Telling it to be accurate does nothing, and makes errors sound more convincing.
  • Asking for sources is worthless unless you follow them.

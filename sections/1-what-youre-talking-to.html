What you're actually talking to

Almost everything that goes wrong with these tools follows from one fact about how they work. It takes about five minutes to understand, and it makes the rest of this module mostly common sense.

In this section

  • Why these systems produce fluent text rather than looked-up facts.
  • Why confidence tells you nothing about accuracy.
  • Why the chatbot you use and the model inside it are not the same thing.

It is predicting, not looking up

A large language model is trained by being shown an enormous amount of text and repeatedly asked to predict what comes next. Over billions of these attempts it gets extremely good at producing continuations that fit the patterns of the text it learned from.

That is the whole mechanism. When you ask a question, it is not consulting a store of facts and returning the matching one. It is generating the text most likely to follow your question, given everything it absorbed during training.

This explains something people find genuinely puzzling: why a system can be brilliant on one question and confidently wrong on the next, with no change in tone. If a fact appears constantly and consistently in written text — the capital of Nevada, the plot of a famous novel — then the likely continuation and the true one are the same thing, and you get a reliable answer. If a topic is obscure, contested, or very recent, likely and true come apart. The output looks identical either way, because it was produced the same way either way.

The part worth remembering

Fluency and accuracy are produced by the same process, so neither one is evidence of the other. A confident, well-organised, beautifully written answer is not more likely to be true than a hesitant one. It is just better-formed text.

The model and the product are different things

This distinction matters more every year, and most confusion about “is AI reliable?” comes from collapsing it.

The model is the thing described above: it answers from patterns learned during training, and its knowledge stops wherever its training data stopped. The product is everything built around that model — a search tool that fetches current web pages, a retrieval system that reads your uploaded documents, code that runs calculations, an interface that shows sources.

These fail in different ways, and knowing which you are using changes what you should check:

Two different systems, two different failure modes
If it is answering from training alone If it is searching and retrieving
It may invent a source that does not exist, because a plausible citation is a plausible continuation. The sources are usually real and really were retrieved.
It has no access to anything after its training cutoff. It can be current, but may lean on whatever ranked highly today.
Check whether the thing it cited exists at all. Check whether the real source it cited actually says this.

Section 2 is mostly about that second column, because it is the one people are least prepared for.

“Thinking” is also generated text

Many tools now show their working — a visible chain of intermediate steps, often labelled reasoning or thinking. This genuinely does improve results on problems with several steps, and it is useful to see.

But those steps are generated the same way the answer is. They are not a proof, and they are not a log of an internal process you are being shown a window into. A chain of steps can look meticulous and still arrive somewhere wrong, and a plausible-looking chain is exactly as easy to produce as a plausible-looking citation. Read it as a useful indication of where the reasoning might be weak, not as verification.

Where the training data came from

Worth knowing, because it is part of what you are using. These models were trained on very large amounts of text scraped from the web and from published works, much of it copyrighted, and the legal questions about that are still being argued in court. The training process also depends on substantial human labour — people reviewing and rating output, often low-paid and frequently exposed to distressing material. And training and running these systems consumes meaningful amounts of electricity and water, which is why data centre siting has become a live issue in Nevada in particular.

None of this means you should not use these tools. It does mean the costs are real and fall on people other than the user, which is worth holding in mind rather than discovering later.

Check yourself

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

You ask for an explanation of a theory from your course. It comes back clear and confident. It is clear and confident about the parts it has wrong too — and those are likelier to be the specific details your instructor cares about than the general outline.

At a service desk

A patron shows you an AI answer about library policy and asks if it is right. The answer sounds authoritative because it is well-written, not because anyone checked. Policy is local, specific, and changes — exactly the conditions where likely and true come apart.

Key takeaways

  • It predicts likely text. It does not look up facts.
  • Confidence and accuracy come from the same process, so one is no evidence of the other.
  • Find out whether your tool is searching or answering from training — they need different checks.
  • Visible “reasoning” is generated text, not proof.

When it makes things up

The advice you have probably heard is “check that the source exists.” That was the right advice in 2023. It is now the advice most likely to get you into trouble, because it will clear a citation that is real, correctly formatted, and completely misused.

In this section

  • Why fabrication follows directly from how these systems work.
  • How the problem changed once tools started citing real sources.
  • Five verification moves, and which one people skip.

First, a guess

Why it invents things

This follows straight from Section 1. Asked for a source, the system produces the text most likely to follow — and what follows a request for a citation is something that looks like a citation. A real journal name. A real author who works in the field. A volume number consistent with the year. Page numbers in a normal range.

Every component is drawn from patterns in real citations, which is exactly why the result is convincing. It is not a corrupted memory of a real article. It is a well-formed citation-shaped object, assembled because citation-shaped objects are what follow that kind of request.

The word you will see for this is “hallucination.” It is worth knowing because it is everywhere, but it is a poor description: it suggests a perceptual glitch, something going briefly wrong in a system that otherwise perceives correctly. Nothing went wrong. The system did the only thing it does. Confabulation is closer — producing a fluent, confident account with no underlying record behind it — and plainest of all is simply saying it made it up.

What changed, and why the old advice fails

Most tools now search the web, retrieve documents, and show sources. This genuinely helped: wholesale invention of citations is much less common than it was.

But it moved the problem rather than solving it. The failure now is a real source, correctly cited, that does not say what the answer claims it says. A paper gets cited for a finding it never reported. A modest result gets stretched into a sweeping one. A study of one population is presented as a general fact.

This is harder to catch than invention was, for a reason worth sitting with: everything looks right. The link works. The journal is prestigious. The authors are real. The citation survives every check except the one that matters — and the presence of a working link actively discourages people from running it. Retrieval made sources checkable. It did not make them checked.

AI-generated response

Research on first-year students' source evaluation has grown considerably over the past decade. 1 Nguyen (2021) found that students who received embedded library instruction were substantially more likely to consult peer-reviewed sources than those who did not (College & Research Libraries, 82(3), 411–428). 2 This aligns with broader findings that roughly 68% of first-year students cannot reliably distinguish a scholarly article from a popular one. 3 Wineburg and McGrew (2019) demonstrated that lateral reading — leaving a page to check what other sources say about it — is a markedly more effective strategy than close reading of the page itself. 4 As Head and Eisenberg's Project Information Literacy work established, students who use library databases go on to earn higher course grades. 5 A 2024 meta-analysis in the Journal of Academic Librarianship synthesised 340 studies on information literacy instruction (doi:10.1016/j.acalib.2024.102847).

Five moves that actually work

Named, because a named move is one you can remember at 11pm.

  1. Go to the container, not the citation. Do not try to verify the citation as a unit — every part of it will check out. Open the journal issue and look at what is actually on those pages.
  2. Resolve the DOI. Paste it into doi.org. Ten seconds, and it catches the common case of a real identifier attached to the wrong article.
  3. Read what the source says. The highest-value move and the one almost everyone skips. Open it and find the sentence that supports the claim. If you cannot find it, that is your answer.
  4. Read laterally. Do not just interrogate the source in front of you — leave it and see what others say about the same claim. This is the Wineburg and McGrew finding in claim 3, which is real.
  5. Use the Libraries as ground truth. Our databases are curated records of what was actually published. That is a different kind of thing from a generated answer, and it is the thing to check against. Ask a librarian if you are stuck.

Try one for real

Optional, and worth the two minutes. Take claim 5's DOI — 10.1016/j.acalib.2024.102847 — and paste it into doi.org in a new tab. Note what happens, and note how long it took. That is the entire cost of the check people skip.

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

You have five citations and a deadline. Checking all five properly takes about ten minutes. Not checking them can cost you the assignment and a conversation you very much do not want to have.

At a service desk

Someone brings you citations that do not resolve. The useful thing is not only finding real sources — it is one sentence explaining that the tool does this, so they are not left thinking they did something wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Fabricated citations are assembled from real parts, so checking any single part confirms them.
  • The current failure is real sources misrepresented, not invented ones.
  • “Does it exist?” is no longer enough. “Does it say this?” is the question.
  • One correct claim in five: the goal is calibration, not blanket suspicion.

What not to paste

Lists of banned items go out of date and nobody remembers them anyway. One question does most of the work: who else could end up seeing this, and is it mine to share?

In this section

  • What actually happens to text you paste into a chatbot.
  • Which categories of information are not yours to share, and why.
  • What UNLV provides, and what “Enhanced Data Protection” does and does not mean.

First, what do you think happens?

What actually happens

It depends almost entirely on the account and the agreement behind it, which is why the honest answer to “is it safe to paste this?” is usually “into what?”

With a personal consumer account, your input is typically retained, may be reviewed by staff for quality or safety, and may be used to improve the service. The terms can change, and the company has no obligations to you or to UNLV beyond whatever you agreed to.

With a tool the university holds a contract for, there is an agreement specifying retention, access, and whether inputs may be used for training. UNLV provides Rebel Spark AI (Claude) free to students, faculty and staff, with Enhanced Data Protection — meaning conversations are not used to train models. Several other tools are available through departmental funding.

Available on campus is not the same as protected

Do not assume that anything reachable with your UNLV login carries the same terms. Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat, for instance, is broadly available here and does not have Enhanced Data Protection. Check which tool you are actually in before deciding what goes into it — the campus AI technology page lists what applies to which.

The categories that are not yours

Protection is about the destination. Permission is about the information. Enhanced Data Protection improves the first and does nothing for the second, which is where most mistakes happen.

  • Education records — anything identifying a student alongside their academic or account information. This is FERPA territory, and it is not yours to share regardless of how good the tool's terms are.
  • Patron information — what someone read, asked, or borrowed. Confidential under Article III of the ALA Code of Ethics, which library workers hold to independently of any institutional policy.
  • Licensed content — database articles and ebooks come with terms limiting how much you may extract and where it may go. Uploading substantial portions can put the agreement at risk for the whole campus.
  • Unpublished work entrusted to you — a manuscript, a grant draft, a student paper you are reviewing. Shared with you, not with a company.
  • Other people's personal information, in general. Your judgment does not extend to their data.
  • Your own sensitive information — health, finances, immigration status. This one is genuinely your call, and worth making deliberately rather than by habit.

What to do instead

Rarely “give up.” Usually one of: remove the identifying details before you paste; describe the problem in your own words rather than pasting the source; work from a short quotation instead of the whole document; or use a tool with an institutional agreement.

One caution on de-identifying: it is harder than find-and-replace. A narrated incident with every name removed can still point to one person on a campus this size. Read what is left and ask whether a colleague could work out who it is.

Nine things you might be tempted to paste

Two situations, one principle

Writing a paper

Your own draft is yours to share. A group project draft contains your collaborators' work, and they have not agreed to anything. Ask them, which takes a message.

At a service desk

A patron's question is theirs, not yours, even when helping them would be easier with AI assistance. Reword it yourself, or work from the general shape of the problem.

Key takeaways

  • Ask one question: who else could see this, and is it mine to share?
  • Protection is about the destination; permission is about the information. They are separate.
  • UNLV provides Rebel Spark AI free with Enhanced Data Protection. Not every campus tool has it.
  • De-identifying is a judgment call, not a find-and-replace.

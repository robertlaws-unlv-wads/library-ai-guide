The four questions

For any situation nobody has written a rule for:

  1. Can I check it? If you cannot verify the output, you cannot use it responsibly.
  2. Does the judgment belong to me? Is this the part of the work you are supposed to be doing?
  3. Is the input mine to share? Your judgment does not extend to other people's data.
  4. Am I allowed, and will I say so? Permission and disclosure are separate requirements.

How it works, in one line

It predicts likely text rather than looking up facts. Fluency and accuracy come out of the same process, so a confident answer is not a more reliable one. Find out whether your tool is searching the web or answering from training — they fail differently.

Verifying a claim

  1. Go to the container, not the citation. Open the journal issue and read the contents.
  2. Resolve the DOI at doi.org. Ten seconds.
  3. Read what the source says. The one people skip, and the one that catches the modern failure.
  4. Read laterally. Leave the page and see what others say about the claim.
  5. Check against the Libraries' databases. A record of what was actually published.

“Does this source exist?” is no longer the question. “Does this source say this?” is.

Before you paste

Ask: who else could end up seeing this, and is it mine to share?

Not yours to share: education records with student identifiers; what a patron read, asked or borrowed; licensed database and ebook content; unpublished work entrusted to you; other people's personal information.

UNLV provides Rebel Spark AI (Claude) free to students, faculty and staff, with Enhanced Data Protection. Not everything reachable with your UNLV login carries the same terms — Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat does not. Protection is about the destination; permission is about the information. Neither substitutes for the other.

Permission, disclosure, citation

  • Permission — your syllabus or your supervisor decides. There is no single campus rule.
  • Disclosure — one specific sentence about what the tool did. Usually required even when nothing was generated.
  • Citation — only when AI-generated content appears in your work.

Prompting

Add information the system did not have, or create something you can check. Narrow the question, say who it is for and in what form, say what you already know, give an example, ask for sources and then follow them.

Telling it to be accurate does nothing. Nor does offering it a tip.

If a detector accuses you or someone you are helping

The score is not evidence, and these tools misfire far more often on multilingual writers. Move the conversation from the percentage to the process: drafts, notes, outlines, version history.

Where to get help

Ask a Librarian for research and verification questions. AI 101 for more background on how these systems work. UNLV IT for which tools are licensed and what protections they carry.
